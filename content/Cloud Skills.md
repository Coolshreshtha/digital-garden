Not too long ago, I made a living working as a Technology Consultant specializing in Cloud Technologies for enterprise scale in various industries. So in this note, I'll refer back to that experience as a way to jog my own memory, as well as define a sort of path for anyone wanting to figure out exactly what a career in the space might entail.

### Kitchen for rent
My favorite analogy here is  of course, food related. If I want to make a nice curry dinner, I first need to set-up a kitchen with all the pots and pans and ingredients (think setting up a warehouse and all the employees needed). This is the fully DIY end of the spectrum, and the other end is to order takeout (contract someone to do it for me). The space in the middle of these two extremes can be solved with cloud computing!

I would have to rent a kitchen (which would correspond to a cloud provider like AWS, Azure, GCP, etc.). Then, my approach would depend on my much I know about making a curry and naan, as well as how much time I've got and how much I can afford to spend.  I can choose to only pay for the ingredients I use, where I grind my own spices and make my own dough for the naans.  Or, at a higher cost, I can use their ready to use dough and curry pastes. I can pay an even higher cost to  use their ready to bake naans and curry mix that only needs hot water.  The levels roughly correspond to Infrastructure, Platform or Software as a Service.


### Learning Plan

1. [This article by FreeCodeCamp contributer, Akande Olalekan Toheeb](https://www.freecodecamp.org/news/how-to-start-a-cloud-computing-career/) does a good job of outlining a bunch of common cloud job titles as well as what each entails. 

2. My initial learning in the space was through a work-sponsored Udacity DevOps Nanodegree, and I find this [free coursera course from Duke](https://www.coursera.org/learn/cloud-computing-foundations-duke) a good free first course in terms of covering all the steps from start to finish but not being too overwhelming with details. It also has mini-project exercises, which are really where the real learning happens. I don't see any value in paying for the verified certificate though, the audit should be more than sufficient for our purposes.

3. Once you get a sense of how a basic project looks from start to finish, it is helpful to see increasing levels of complexity. In consulting, this was basically being thrown into a real world project. I really like [this video from AWS about how Majid Al Futtaim uses AWS ](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9qTEHITVeLE) , and there are plenty more videos in this series that show different industries, use cases, needs and what the thinking process behind those were. The key skills here are knowing the birds eye view of how the whole system connects and functions (This was my ABSOLUTE FAVORITE part).

In everyday work, especially in early career roles, most of your work would not be in the architecture side, but rather in building it out, bit by bit. This looks more like your traditional software engineer role, with sprints and tickets and the usual code writing and debugging etc. Depending on what area you choose to focus on, your daily work would look probably like some sort of scripting. It could be writing YAML or JSON scripts for DevOps, python scripts for lambda functions,  configuring Kubernetes or docker systems for at-scale deployments, etc. 

Things gets more and more removed from these basic concepts as you go into a niche like a Kubernetes etc.  I'm less familiar with that because by the nature of my job as a consultant, I was thrown into a bunch of very distinct projects. This was somehow the best and worst part of the job; best because I got to touch a lot of things; worst because I lack depth in any one of areas, as is expected from someone with 3-4 yrs in the field.  But building up that expertise shouldn't be too hard, given that I once interviewed (and got an offer) for a Platform Engineer role that was very Kubernetes heavy, and built up most of the required skills depth while prepping for the interviews (I had done some Kubernetes work before).  Being able to contextualize your expertise and form a narrative around your career helps a ton here as well, but [[Interviewing for Tech]] is a whole another note in itself!
