Economists love models, and Statistics and Math are the language we use to wax poetic about them. Let's dive into the basics!

Think of Statistics as using numbers to paint a picture. The goal here is to  understand, describe or predict a situation. This involves getting our hands on some data, formulating our questions, then wrangling the data to get as close to our answers as we can, using the variety of tools we have at our disposal.


### Random Sampling
It is usually impossible to truly check every single possible instance of a situation. We try to do our best by picking a truly **random sample** that is independent and identically distributed, and then use the result to estimate for the actual info (or population). I say estimate because, say, the average height of a 100 people doesn't tell us the height of the entire population of humans, but repeated enough times, the average of our sample starts to be more likely to be closer to the actual average height of all humans (Also known as the law of Large numbers.)

### Asking the right questions
It is hard to overstate the importance of formulating good, relevant and meaningful questions. These questions determine the significance of our answers and what methods and tools we should use.

#### Descriptive Statistics
When our goal is to get a good sense of a situation, descriptive statistics is our tool of choice. A sample of data is like a snapshot of the larger picture. We can look at it's shape and key features to understand what exactly is going on, without poring over every single datapoint. Some of the most common ones are central tendency and shape.

Central tendencies are the "briefing points" of the dataset, such as how the data is spread and what the locations of key indicators are, like the average (mean), the mid point (median) or the most frequent values (mode).
The shape of data plotted on a graph can tell us a lot about how the data is spread. Through measures like Variance and Standard deviation (that tell us how far data values deviate from the mean), skewness (how asymmetric our data is), or kurtosis (degree of outliers present in the dataset). 

We can also think about the likelihood of certain outcomes by using tools like cumulative distribution functions or probability distribution functions. Furthermore, depending on our questions, it makes sense to group our data into categories (red,blue,tall etc), intervals (0-10, 100-200), or continuous data (like height= 1.67cm etc)

Once we start to look at more than one variable, we can even use tools like covariance and correlation coefficients to see if there is a relationship between the movement of distinct variables. In advance Statistics, a tool of choice to quantify relationships between two variables is Regression.

Another major area of Statistics is Inferential Statistics, or the tools we can use with our data to makes inferences or predictions about things. That will be a topic for another article.
