This past Thursday, I had a code "exam" for my Data Science class. I did the exam with very little help from AI tools, and certainly without offloading cognitive tasks to it. Needless to say, it was a fair bit of work and very tiring. But it also felt very satisfying.

Right after, I was hanging out with some friends over lunch, and the topic of using AI tools came up. We talked about how some of the people were leaving the exam room within 30 mins, and I'm sure at least 20 of those were spent trying to look busy, after they had asked chatgpt to generate the code for them. My friend asked why I chose to do the exam by myself, when coding with LLM tools was the smart thing to do. In that moment I couldn't articulate why I wanted to do the assignment without the AI tools, unsurprisingly given I just came out of the aforementioned exhausting exam. I think I have a better answer now. 

Over the past few years, I've come to realise that there are certain things that I value highly in life. Those certain things that bring me good feelings and satisfaction, as outlined in [[Things that I value in my work]]. Psychologists much more articulate than me describe it as a combination of [[Drive by Daniel Pink|autonomy, mastery and purpose]]. Being able to do as much of the exam as I can gave me a sense of mastery, as I used a skill I have spent a lot of [[Undergrad in Computer Science|trying to master]]. It was very satisfying to go through the whole process of doing something difficult that I wasn't capable of before. While using AI tools to help debug or give me the syntax of certain R functions would aid that process, asking it to solve a question, or even to list out all the steps, would take away from that satisfaction.

I, of course, empathise with the folks who did leave the exam room in 20 min. You stand on your tip-toes to touch a low tree branch, but it's insane to think that is enough to think it's useful for touching one of those high and fancy chandeliers in a ball room. We only stretch ourselves when it seems like the gap to cover is reasonable. For a lot of the folks who did outsource their exam to the LLM, they did so because it is impossible to learn to code from scratch in 5 weekly lectures of 2 hours each, with very little, if any, hands on keyboard time. 

I too sometimes use tools like Claude and NotebookLM for my Economics classes, where I learn the material as it guides me through the problem set. Often, these problem sets are passing through my hands faster than my brain can absorb all this material. I'm learning both my masters subject matter, and filling in for an undergrad in Econ, with some more specific life circumstances peppered in. AI tools are often the super helpful and patient tutor I desperately need. Sure, given enough time, I know I am capable of figuring all of this on my own, and some say [[Deliberate Practice|marinating in the sheer hard work of problem solving is how the real learning happens]]. But I also have a grad program to pass. We offload the hard work to AI cognition when the world moves just too fast for the human one. 

Moreover, there are certain things that AI is downright better at. As a part of a career workshop the other day, I asked an LLM service to generate job profile suggestions for me (in incognito mode ofc). It came up with a lot of ideas that I wasn't even remotely aware of. I don't have a reasonable estimate of how long it would have taken my brain to come up with such a list, because it would have required me to be exposed to a whole bunch of information that I don't yet have a link or exposure to. In a way, this is akin to how a dating app exposes you to folks who have the potential to be a good fit for you, but you have no mutuals with them. I suppose this is a use case I would go back to AI for, and indeed I have in the past, as I explored a career transition in the past year or so (and ultimately [[Why I'm studying Economics |decided to study Economics]]).

There are areas of life where I'm more reluctant to use AI. I had a visceral internal reaction when in the same workshop, we were suggested to use AI tools to *copy people's Linkedin profiles (!!!) and use it to generate connection requests and talking points*. One, because I can come up with better stuff if I take 10 seconds to scan the profile instead of 30 to copy and paste things in the bot and then try to see if it makes sense. Two, because that is just *weird*. I can see how this can be beneficial if the goal is to see what it comes up with, with the hopes of one day being able to do this yourself. But if you can't be bothered to look through someone's Linkedin profile, are they really the right person to have a coffee chat with? 

Some might say this is a classic case of "don't hate the player, hate the game". That this is just what a person has to do in [[The balance between using AI and getting value from Ai|a career market that has been inundated with AI slop and AI arms race]], and there's a lot of truth to that. But we have to be wary. Human brain is designed such that it is *really* difficult to resist an easier path in the favor of a more challenging one, even if you know the virtues of the latter, and especially when you are in a difficult situation. The more you rely on the tools for coming up with an answer, the less practice your own "model" gets, and what you don't use, you lose. 

For instance, as I mentioned before, studying for my [[Graduate School|grad program in Economics]] is really hard. I often take help from AI in the process because I don't have the luxury of time to fully absorb the material given how frantic the pace of my one year program is (even people who have much more aligned backgrounds are struggling to keep up at times). I often question if I would have gotten more out of the program if I took it before the AI tools existed (or if I would have flunked out already). I'm still trying to decide on the personal rubric of when AI use is acceptable and in line with my goals of from the program vs. not. For instance, asking NotebookLM to generate a podcast out of my slides (acceptable), asking it to come up with the next step in a math-based proof (eh, in real life, a friend or a tutor would guide me to come up with the right answer, as I did in my own role as a class mentor at Grinnell), etc. To make matters more complicated, there is the whole question of whether it is essential to keep up with the new ways of dong things, and what the nature of my future tasks will be like.

But we all have to draw the line somewhere. My arbitrary line in the sand for now is, 
* ***don't make AI do things that I do better**. I won't use an AI tool to generate questions about a coffee chat or some person on Linkedin. 
* **I will use it to aid, rather than hinder my brain** For instance, I will fight fire with fire and use it as an effective web search to cut through the information glut rife with AI slop, and all the overwhelm that causes to my brain. Or much like how we use documentation while writing code, I will use AI so that it helps me take the misery out of debugging, while doing all the cognitive and problem solving heavy lift myself. 
* **I will limit it's use in things that I hope to build as a skill** such as being articulate and creative.  The brain in brainstorming will be my own whenever possible, but I might turn to it for coming up with unknown unknowns. When i do turn to AI-storming it will have to be as a support or reference, such that I do the bulk of the cognition myself.
* More to come..

Nothing learnt ever goes to waste. Learning a skill is an inherently satisfying process (when done in the right conditions). So, I will try and continue to learn difficult things, because for me, that might be the key to a satisfying life.
